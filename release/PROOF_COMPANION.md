## Theorem 2.2

- source: `02_s2_finite_fibre_height_and_cardinality.tex`  
- head: finite-fibre cardinality; `FiniteFiberCardinality.lean`, `singleQuadratic_finiteFiber_cardinality_vector`

**Statement.** With the same P, there is C ∈ ℕ such that simultaneously {FiberHeightBoundVecZZ}\ P\ C and {FiberCardBoundVecZZ}\ P\ C hold, the latter meaning: for N ≥ 1 and ‖a‖_{∞} ≤ N, a finite fibre has at most (2CN^{2} + 1)^{m} elements. The constant is the same C (the cardinality bound is the box count of the height bound: `fiberCardBoundVec_of_fiberHeightBoundVec`).

**Proof.** We first treat the scalar case m = 1 (`singleQuadratic_finiteFiber_height_scalar`) and then reduce the vector case to it.

Fix P ∈ {Poly} (n+1) r of total degree at most two and a parameter point a ∈ ℤ^{n}. Relabel the variables so that the output coordinate c and the r unknowns u form one block of r+1 variables and the parameters form the other (`relabelG`, `eval_relabelG_ZZ`); the relabelled polynomial has total degree at most two, since renaming variables cannot raise the degree. Splitting it into homogeneous components in the block (c, u) and specialising the parameters at a gives

P(a, c, u) = H(c,u) + Σ_{i} ℓ_{i}(a)·x_{i} + q(a),

where H is the homogeneous quadratic component in the r+1 variables x = (c, u) (possibly the zero polynomial), each ℓᵢ is a polynomial of degree at most one in a, and q has degree at most two in a (`normal_form_general_quadratic`). The point of the decomposition is that H does not depend on a: as the parameter point moves, only the affine part of the fibre equation moves, and it moves polynomially.

Now apply the arithmetic projection dichotomy of Section 9 (Theorem 9.2) to the homogeneous form H. It supplies a constant K, depending on H alone, such that for every affine datum (ℓ, q) the set of natural numbers c admitting an integer witness u with H(c,u) + ℓ·(c,u) + q = 0 is either infinite or bounded above by

{fboundG} K ℓ q = K·((1 + Σ_{i}|ℓ_{i}|)^{2} + |q|).

Applying this to H, and to its reflection in the output coordinate (`flipZero H`, still homogeneous of degree two), covers the negative half of the fibre as well; the two constants are combined into one. If the fibre is finite, the infinite horn of the dichotomy is impossible on both halves, so every c in the fibre satisfies |c| ≤ {fboundG} (K_{1}+K_{2}) ℓ(a) q(a).

It remains to bound that expression in terms of N. Each ℓᵢ has degree at most one and q degree at most two in a, so on the box ‖a‖_{∞} ≤ N their values are bounded by SL·N and SQ·N² for constants SL, SQ depending only on the coefficient sizes of P (`eval_abs_le_int`), whence {fboundG} (K_{1}+K_{2}) ℓ(a) q(a) ≤ C·N^{2} with C := (K_{1}+K_{2})·((1+SL)^{2} + SQ) (`fboundG_box_le`). This proves the scalar case, and the constant depends only on P.

For the vector case, fix a coordinate j and consider the polynomial obtained by keeping the j-th output coordinate as the output and moving the remaining m−1 output coordinates into the block of unknowns (`coordPoly`). This does not change the total degree, and a point b of the vector fibre yields, for each j, a point b_{j} of the scalar fibre of coordPoly j (`mem_fiberZZ_coordPoly`). A finite vector fibre has finite scalar projections, so the scalar bound applies to each coordinate with a constant C_{j}; taking C to be the maximum over the finitely many j gives the statement.

## Remark 2.3

- source: `02_s2_finite_fibre_height_and_cardinality.tex`  
- head: on effectivity

**Statement.** The constant C is not claimed to be effective in Theorem 2.1, and the proof above shows precisely where effectivity would have to come from. The parameter-dependent part is completely explicit: SL and SQ are read off the coefficients of P, and the passage from fboundG to C·N² is elementary. The one non-explicit ingredient is K, the constant supplied by the projection dichotomy for the homogeneous form H. The paper proves that K exists; it does not prove an algorithm that computes K, or C, from the coefficients of P, and no such algorithm is claimed. We therefore state {theorem:2.1,theorem:2.2,theorem:3.2,theorem:4.1} with a non-effective constant, and we do not use effectivity anywhere. Nothing in Sections 6 and 7 depends on it, and it is worth separating four things that are easy to run together. The finite-fibre theorem gives a non-effective structural bound. The computability of SE₂-definable *total functions* (Theorem 7.1) uses neither that bound nor any external input: totality plus a Diophantine graph makes a dovetailed search terminate. The recursiveness of SE₂ *relations* (Section 6) uses Definition 1.5, since positive search settles membership but not its complement. Uniformity in the code is not a convenience there: a decision procedure supplied one equation at a time would give each fixed relation as recursive, but the diagonalization of Theorem 6.5, which is what separates SE₂ from REC at arity one, quantifies over a coded family and needs the decision to be uniform in the code. The certificates of Section 8 assert the existence of a structured output subset, with witnesses selected non-effectively. A non-effective bound terminates no search, and we nowhere ask it to.

## Theorem 3.3

- source: `03_s3_definable_total_functions.tex`  
- head: `singleQuadratic_total_function_computable`, `ppQuadratic_total_function_computable`

**Statement.** SE₂- and PP₂-definable total functions are computable (witness search on the graph, then read off the unique output).

**Proof.** Let f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m} be total with graphVecZZ f ∈ SE_{2}(n+m), represented by P with witness arity r: (a, b) ∈ {graph} ⟺ ∃u ∈ ℤ^{r}, P(a, b, u) = 0. ## Theorem 3.4

- source: `03_s3_definable_total_functions.tex`  
- head: composition; `ppQuadratic_functions_closed_under_composition`, `singleQuadratic_functions_not_closed_under_composition`

**Statement.** PP₂-definable functions are closed under composition (the intermediate vector is carried as fresh witnesses, `compSys`). SE_{2}-definable functions are not: x ↦ x^{2} is SE_{2}-definable but x ↦ x^{4} is not, since quartic growth contradicts Theorem 3.2. The ℤ → ℤ instance is `UnarySE2ZZ`.

**Proof.** *Closure for PP₂.* Let f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m} and g : ℤ^{m} → ℤᵖ have PP₂-definable graphs, by systems S_{f} in witnesses u and S_{g} in witnesses v. Then

(a, c) ∈ {graph}(g ∘ f) ⟺ ∃ b ∈ ℤ^{m}, ∃u, ∃v : S_{f}(a, b, u) = 0 ∧ S_{g}(b, c, v) = 0,

and the right-hand side is a single system: no equation is altered, the intermediate vector b is carried as fresh existential witnesses alongside u and v, and every equation still has total degree at most two. So {graph}(g ∘ f) ∈ PP_{2}(n+p). The step that a system permits and a single equation does not is exactly this conjunction: combining two quadratics into one equation by summing their squares would produce total degree four.

*Failure for SE₂.* The map x ↦ x^{2} is SE₂-definable: its graph is cut out by b − a^{2} = 0, one equation of total degree two with no witnesses. If Fun(SE₂) were closed under composition, x ↦ x^{4} would be SE₂-definable, and Theorem 3.2 would give a constant C with |a^{4}| ≤ C(1+|a|)^{2} for every a — false for |a| large. So closure fails, and it fails at arity one (`UnarySE2ZZ`).

## Theorem 4.1

- source: `04_s4_injective_dimension_theorem.tex`  
- head: `singleQuadratic_injective_dimension_sharp`

**Statement.** If f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m} is {SE}_{2}-definable and injective then n ≤ m.

**Proof.** Write the defining equation, after renaming the variables, as Q(z) + ℓ_{a}(z) + q(a) = 0, where z carries the output and the unknowns, Q is an integer quadratic form in z alone, ℓ_{a} is linear in z with coefficients affine in a, and q is quadratic in a. Over ℚ the form Q admits five behaviours, exhaustive but not pairwise exclusive (`branch_exhaustive`), and each forces one of the two structural alternatives of Theorem 4.2: either f has *linear* growth, ‖f(a)‖_{∞} ≤ C(1 + ‖a‖_{∞}), or f agrees with a vector of rational polynomials of degree at most two on one full lattice coset ρ + Mℤ^{n}. In the first case the box ‖a‖_{∞} ≤ N has (2N+1)^{n} points whose images lie in a box of side 2C(1+N)+1, so injectivity gives (2N+1)^{n} ≤ (2C(1+N)+1)^{m} for every N, and comparing growth in N forces n ≤ m. In the second case counting does not suffice: the images lie in a box of side quadratic in N, and the count alone yields only n ≤ 2m (Remark 4.5). Suppose instead n > m, and write the restriction to the coset as f(ρ + Mt) = (p_{1}(t), …, p_{m}(t)) with p_{j} = c_{j} + L_{j} + H_{j}, where L_{j} is linear and H_{j} is homogeneous quadratic over ℚ. The linear map L = (L_{1}, …, L_{m}) : ℚ^{n} → ℚ^{m} has a nonzero kernel because m < n; clearing denominators gives v ∈ ℤ^{n} {0} with L(v) = 0. Since H_{j}(-v) = H_{j}(v), p_{j}(v) - p_{j}(-v) = 2L_{j}(v) = 0 for every j, so f(ρ + Mv) = f(ρ - Mv) while ρ + Mv ≠ ρ - Mv because M > 0 and v ≠ 0, contradicting injectivity. Hence n ≤ m. No hypothesis on the discriminant, on a common centre of the quadratic parts, or on the number of unknowns is used (`exists_collision_vector`, `not_injective_of_weakVectorCosetNF`).

## Remark 4.6

- source: `04_s4_injective_dimension_theorem.tex`  
- head: what is due to whom at n = 2, m = 1, no unknowns

**Statement.** When the defined function is itself a polynomial, the case n = 2, m = 1 with no unknowns is Lew and Rosenberg [LR78a], Thm.~5.3(2): no quadratic storing function exists on ℤ^{2}. Their statement is about polynomials, and their three-line proof uses only integer-valuedness. Neither reaches the present statement, because a function with {SE}_{2}-definable graph need not be a polynomial: the equation 4b^{2} - (4a_{1}+2)b + (a_{1}^{2} + a_{1}) = 0 is total and output-unique over ℤ and defines a ↦ ⌈a_{1}/2⌉, which is not a polynomial of any degree (`parity_exists_unique`; the formalised part is `parity_not_quadratic`, which rules out rational polynomials of degree at most two). ## Theorem 5.1

- source: `05_s5_polynomial_graph_classification.tex`  
- head: `MultivariatePolynomialGraphs.lean`, `polynomial_graph_singleQuadratic_iff`

**Statement.** For every n and every p ∈ ℤ[x_{1},…,x_{n}]: {graph}(p) ∈ {SE}_{2}(n+1) ⟺ {totalDegree}\ p ≤ 2. Here {totalDegree} is mathlib's, so the zero polynomial has degree 0 and falls under the left-hand side; no separate convention is adopted for it.

**Proof.** (⇐) The graph is cut out by the single equation y − p = 0, of total degree at most two. (⇒) Suppose {deg} p = d ≥ 3. Write p = p_{d} + (lower), with p_{d} the top homogeneous part, which is nonzero. Since ℤ is infinite, p_{d} does not vanish identically, so there is v ∈ ℤ^{n} with p_{d}(v) ≠ 0 (`exists_eval_ne_zero`). Restrict p to the line t ↦ tv: the resulting univariate polynomial has degree exactly d and leading coefficient p_{d}(v) (`linePoly_natDegree`, `exists_line_natDegree_eq_totalDegree`), so |p(tv)| grows like |t|^{d}. On the other hand a ↦ p(a) would be an SE₂-definable total function, hence of quadratic growth by Theorem 3.2, so |p(tv)| ≤ C(1 + ‖tv‖_{∞})^{2} = C(1 + |t|·‖v‖_{∞})^{2} for all t; for |t| ≥ 1 this is at most C(1 + ‖v‖_{∞})² t², so |p(tv)| = O(t^{2}) — a contradiction for large t since d ≥ 3.

## Theorem 6.4

- source: `06_s6_relation_phase_diagram.tex`  
- head: code bridge; `QuadraticCodes.lean`, `GSInputCompiler.lean`, `GSCompilerComputable.lean`

**Statement.** (a) Evaluation of a code at (parameters, unknown list) is primitive recursive uniformly in the code (`quadraticCode_eval_primrec`). (b) The polynomial of a code has total degree at most two (`quadraticCode_toPoly_totalDegree_le_two`) and evaluates as the computable evaluation (`quadraticCode_toPoly_eval`). (c) Every polynomial of total degree at most two is the polynomial of a code (`quadraticCode_surjective`). (d) UniformQuadraticSolvabilityOnCodes ⇒ QuadraticSolvabilityDecidableZZ (`uniformCodeGS_implies_perEquationGS`). (e) The flat form, in which a code is carried as a single natural number rather than as a pair, is equivalent (`uniformCodeGS_iff`, via `codeOfNat`/`natOfCode`). (f) The *input compiler* that folds a parameter list into the coefficient table, producing a parameter-free equation with the same integer solutions, is primitive recursive (`globalGS_input_compiler_computable`, `globalInstance_eval_iff`); consequently the reduction from our instance form to the parameter-free coefficient-table form of the literature is effective, not merely extensional.

**Proof.** Throughout, a code is a pair c = (r, T): a witness arity r ∈ ℕ and a finite list T of terms, each term a coefficient α ∈ ℤ together with at most two variable codes. Evaluation at parameters a ∈ ℤ^{k} and unknowns u ∈ ℤ^{r} reads a variable code i as the i-th entry of the environment `ofFn a ++ u`, an out-of-range code as 1 and an absent factor as 1, and sums the terms.

*(a).* Fix the environment map e(a, u) := `ofFn a ++ u`. The value of a single term (α, i?, j?) is α · e(a,u)[i] · e(a,u)[j] with the two conventions above, and the value of the code is the fold of those values over T. Every ingredient is primitive recursive: list indexing with a default and list append are primitive recursive; integer addition and multiplication are; `Option.elim` against the constant 1 is; and primitive recursive functions are closed under composition, pairing and `List.foldr`. The code c occurs as an *argument* of the composite, not as a parameter of the construction, so the resulting map (c, a, u) ↦ {evalZZ} c a u is primitive recursive uniformly in c, which is the content of the clause. That the type of codes is `Primcodable` is what makes "uniformly in c" a statement about a single function rather than a family.

*(b).* Define toPoly c as the sum over T of the monomials α·X_{i}·X_{j}, an absent or out-of-range factor contributing the constant 1. Each summand is a monomial in at most two variables, hence of total degree at most two, and the total degree of a sum is at most the maximum of the total degrees; so {totalDegree} (toPoly c) ≤ 2. Agreement with the computable evaluation is an induction on T: polynomial evaluation is additive over the sum, and on a single term both sides compute α · (value of the first factor) · (value of the second factor) under the same two conventions. The empty list gives 0 on both sides.

*(c).* Let P be a polynomial in k + r variables with {totalDegree} P ≤ 2. Its monomial expansion is finite and unique, and each monomial of degree at most two is 1, X_{i}, or X_{i} X_{j} with i ≤ j. Map the expansion term by term: a constant monomial α becomes the term (α, none, none); α X_{i} becomes (α, some i, none); α X_{i} X_{j} becomes (α, some i, some j). Let T_{P} be the resulting list and c_{P} := (r, T_{P}). By (b), toPoly c_{P} is the sum of exactly those monomials, i.e. P. The map is total because the support of P is finite, and it needs no choice: the monomial expansion of a polynomial is unique. Surjectivity is what lets a *coded* family range over all degree-two equations, which is the hypothesis (i) of the diagonalization Theorem 6.5 uses.

*(d).* Assume UniformQuadraticSolvabilityOnCodes and let P of total degree at most two with witness arity r be given. By (c) pick a code c_{P} with toPoly c_{P} = P; by (b), for every a the equation P(a, ·) = 0 has an integer solution u ∈ ℤ^{r} if and only if c_{P} is solvable at a. The hypothesis says that (c, a) ↦ (c solvable at a) is a computable predicate; the map a ↦ (c_{P}, a) is computable (a constant paired with the identity), and computable predicates are closed under composition with computable maps, so a ↦ (P solvable at a) is computable. That is QuadraticSolvabilityDecidableZZ. Only this direction is claimed. The converse fails to be available for the use we make of it: deciding one equation at a time gives each fixed relation as recursive but does not give a single decider uniform in the code, and the diagonalization behind Corollary 6.6 quantifies over the coded family. That is why Definition 1.5 is stated in the uniform form and Theorem 6.3 in the per-equation form, and why the two are kept apart everywhere in Sections 6 and 7.

*(e).* `natOfCode` and `codeOfNat` are the `Primcodable` encoding and decoding of the code type; they are mutually inverse and both primitive recursive by construction of the instance. A computable predicate transports along a computable bijection in both directions, so uniform decidability stated on codes and uniform decidability stated on their numeric encodings are equivalent hypotheses. The clause is bookkeeping, not mathematics; it is recorded because the two forms both occur in the literature and it should be visible that nothing depends on the choice.

*(f).* The equations of [GS04] carry no parameters: the input is a coefficient table. Our instances carry a parameter vector a, so a bridge is required, and the clause asserts that the bridge is effective rather than merely extensional. Define the compiler on terms. A term (α, i?, j?) of a code with k parameters is rewritten by evaluating every factor whose variable code lies in the parameter block, namely in the range i < k: if both factors are parameters the term becomes the constant (α·a_{i}·a_{j}, none, none); if exactly one is, it becomes (α·a_{i}, some j, none) with the unknown index shifted down by k; if neither is, only the shift is applied. Applying this term by term is a `List.map` whose step function is a case split on two `Option` fields together with lookups into `ofFn a`, so the compiler is primitive recursive, and again uniformly in (c, a) because both are arguments. Two properties are immediate from (b) and the definition of evaluation: the compiled code still has at most two variable codes per term, hence total degree at most two; and for every u, evaluating the compiled code at the empty parameter list and u gives the same integer as evaluating c at (a, u), so the two equations have literally the same solution set in u (`globalInstance_eval_iff`). Hence the reduction from our instance form to the parameter-free coefficient-table form is a primitive recursive map of inputs, which is what "effective, not merely extensional" means here.

## Definition 8.2

- source: `08_s8_structured_infinite_fibres.tex`  
- head: the three kinds; `StructuredCertificates.lean`

**Statement.** A *structured certificate* `StructuredCertificate P a` is one of three realizations, each of which carries arithmetic data that *pin* the output map rather than merely asserting that some injection exists.


- *Polynomial progression.* A polynomial q ∈ ℤ[t] with 1 ≤ {deg} q ≤ 2, a threshold t₀, witnesses w_{t}, and proofs that P(a, q(t), w_{t}) = 0 for every t ≥ t_{0} and that q is injective on that tail. The fibre contains an arithmetic or quadratic progression outright.
- *Pell-unit orbit.* A positive nonsquare D, a unit (x₀, y₀) of x² − Dy² = 1 with y_{0} ≠ 0, a base point (X₀, Y₀) of norm N_{0} := X_{0}^{2} − DY_{0}^{2}, and the orbit generated by the unit as an explicit recurrence,


X_{k+1} = x_{0}X_{k} + D y_{0} Y_{k}, Y_{k+1} = y_{0}X_{k} + x_{0}Y_{k},

together with an affine projection with denominator, denom·output_{k} = p_{c} + p_{x} X_{k} + p_{y} Y_{k} with {denom} ≠ 0, witnesses, and injectivity of the output. The norm is preserved along the orbit as a theorem of the data (`PellUnitOrbitRealization.orbit_norm`), not as an extra hypothesis.
- *Moving-modulus step orbit.* Integers b > 0, μ, and a positive *modulus factor* F, a sign ε ∈ {±1}, a sequence c_{k} with moduli t_{k} := b·c_{k} + μ > 0, and primes p_{k} with


F·b·t_{k} < p_{k}, p_{k} ≡ 1 (mod F·b·t_{k}), t_{k+1} = t_{k} · p_{k},

with witnesses for the outputs ε·c_{k}. Strict monotonicity of c_{k} is a consequence of the recurrence and does not have to be assumed (`MovingModulusStepOrbitRealization.c_strictMono`).

## Theorem 8.3

- source: `08_s8_structured_infinite_fibres.tex`  
- head: soundness; `fiber_infinite_of_structuredCertificate`, `StructuredCertificate.toSubset`

**Statement.** Every structured certificate yields a generic certificate and hence proves the fibre infinite; a certified fibre exceeds every bound (`fiber_unbounded_of_certificate`); certificates pull back along integral changes of witness coordinates (`certificate_pullback`), which is what allows a certificate produced in transformed coordinates to be reported in the original ones.

**Proof.** Three assertions, each by the data.

*Every structured certificate yields a generic one.* A generic certificate asks for an infinite index type, an injective output map into ℤ, a witness map, and proofs that each output lies in the fibre with its witness. Each kind of Definition 8.2 supplies exactly these. For a polynomial progression the index type is the tail {t : t ≥ t₀}, which is infinite; the output map is q, injective on that tail by hypothesis; the witnesses are the w_{t}, and P(a, q(t), w_{t}) = 0 is part of the data. For a Pell-unit orbit the index type is ℕ; the output map is determined by {denom}·output_{k} = p_{c} + p_{x} X_{k} + p_{y} Y_{k} with {denom} ≠ 0, and its injectivity is part of the data; the witnesses are again given. For a moving-modulus step orbit the index type is ℕ and the outputs are ε·c_{k}, whose injectivity is not assumed but follows from the recurrence: t_{k+1} = t_{k}·p_{k} with p_{k} > F·b·t_{k} ≥ 1 forces t_{k}, and hence c_{k} = (t_{k} − μ)/b, to increase strictly (`MovingModulusStepOrbitRealization.c_strictMono`). In all three cases forgetting the arithmetic data and keeping the index type, the output map and the witnesses is a generic certificate.

*A certified fibre is infinite, and exceeds every bound.* The output map of a generic certificate is an injection of an infinite type into the fibre, so the fibre contains an infinite set of integers. An infinite set of integers is unbounded in absolute value: a bound B confines it to the finite set [−B, B]. Hence no B bounds the fibre.

*Pull-back along integral changes of witness coordinates.* Let T be an integral matrix with integral inverse and let P′(a, c, u) := P(a, c, Tu). Then u is a witness for P′ at output c precisely when Tu is one for P, and the correspondence u ↦ Tu is a bijection of ℤʳ. Given a certificate for P′, replace each witness w by Tw and keep the index type, the output map and the arithmetic data unchanged; every membership proof transports along the identity P′(a, c, w) = P(a, c, Tw). Injectivity of the output map is untouched because outputs are not changed. This is what allows a certificate produced in transformed coordinates — the coordinates in which the projection dichotomy works — to be reported in the original ones.

## Theorem 8.5

- source: `08_s8_structured_infinite_fibres.tex`  
- head: structured summit, unconditional; `SummitClosure.lean`, `uniformQuadraticFiberStructureZZ_of_quadratic`, `singleQuadratic_fiber_bounded_or_structurallyInfinite`

**Statement.** For every P ∈ {Poly}\ (n+1)\ r with {totalDegree}\ P ≤ 2, {UniformQuadraticFiberStructureZZ}\ P holds. Consequently every scalar fibre of every equation of total degree at most two is bounded by C(1+‖a‖_{∞})^{2} with C depending only on P, or carries a structured certificate of one of the three kinds of Definition 8.2.

**Proof.** The proof has four steps, each of which is a separate theorem in the formalization.

*(i) The uniform constant is free.* For quadratic P the strong target is equivalent to the certificate supply on the infinite side alone: UniformQuadraticFiberStructureZZ P ⟺ (∀ a, fiberZZ P a infinite → the fibre carries a structured certificate) (`uniformQuadraticFiberStructureZZ_iff_infiniteFiberStructured`). One direction is immediate; for the other, take the constant C supplied by Theorem 2.1 in the sup-norm form, and split on whether the fibre is finite. If it is, Theorem 2.1 gives the bound with that C; if it is not, the hypothesis supplies the certificate. So nothing is lost by demanding uniformity, and the whole remaining obligation is the infinite side.

*(ii) Reduction to the nonnegative halves.* An infinite subset of ℤ is infinite on the nonnegative or on the nonpositive side (`infinite_integer_set_pos_or_neg`). Reflecting the output coordinate maps one side to the other, and the reflection acts on certificates kind-preservingly: the polynomial q is replaced by its reflection, the affine projection of a Pell orbit by its negative, and the moving-modulus sign field ε flips (`OutputNegation.lean`, `structuredCertificate_of_flipZero`). It therefore suffices to certify a half-fibre that is infinite, with the sign carried as a parameter ε satisfying ε^{2} = 1.

*(iii) Homogenisation: a half-fibre is the fibre of a homogeneous form.* This is the same decomposition used in the proof of Theorem 2.1, now used to transport structure rather than a bound. Relabelling and splitting into homogeneous components writes the specialised equation as H(x) + ℓ(a)·x + q(a) with H homogeneous of degree two in x = (c, u), and the ε = −1 case is handled by the reflected form (`flipZero H`, `fiberVal_flip`). One obtains H, an integer vector L and an integer Q with

{fiberSetG} H L Q = {m ∈ ℕ | ε·m ∈ {fiberZZ} P a}

(`exists_fiberSetG_eq_fiberNatSigned`). The left-hand side is exactly the object about which Section 9 speaks.

*(iv) The dichotomy in data-carrying form, and the conversion.* Theorem 9.3 below is the projection dichotomy strengthened so that the infinite horn produces not the proposition "this set is infinite" but a *structured family* inside it — a progression, a quadratic progression, a Pell orbit with its recurrence, or a moving-modulus orbit with its primes (`StructuredFamily`, `quadraticProjectionDichotomyZ_all_data`). Transporting such a family along the set equality of step (iii) and converting it into a certificate is then mechanical: choice supplies the witnesses from the membership proofs, the sign ε travels into the polynomial coefficients, into the affine projection, or into the sign field according to the kind, and the derived side conditions of the Pell kind (D > 0, D not a square, y₀ ≠ 0) follow from the unit equation together with x_{0} ≥ 2 (`structuredCertificate_of_family`). If instead the bounded horn holds, the half-fibre is finite, contradicting the assumption of step (ii).

## Theorem 9.3

- head: data-carrying form; `SemanticQuadraticGeneral/TopDichotomyData.lean`, `quadraticProjectionDichotomyZ_all_data`, `quadraticProjectionDichotomyZ_structured`, unconditional

**Statement.** The same statement with "infinite" replaced by "carries a structured family": ∃ K, for every (ℓ, q), either there is a `StructuredFamily (fiberSetG H ℓ q)`, or every element is bounded by {fboundG}\ K\ ℓ\ q. Theorem 9.2 follows by forgetting the family (`dichotomyG_of_dichotomyGData`).

**Proof.** The complete proof of {theorem:9.2,theorem:9.3} is Appendix F of this paper, where it is given as twenty-five numbered lemmas with their proofs; the formal development verifies the same argument, and Section 12 records the correspondence. What follows in this section is a road map of that proof, so that a reader can see the shape of the argument before reading it in full. {theorem:2.1,theorem:8.5} both rest on this one theorem, and a reader is entitled to know what kind of argument carries that weight.

*Normal form.* The quadratic form H is presented by its Gram data, and one first brings it into a shape in which the radical is visible. The reduction uses a unimodular *congruence* U^{T} G U — not a Smith-normal-form left–right equivalence, which would destroy the quadratic form — obtained from the saturation of the integer kernel of G together with a complement of the torsion-free quotient (`RadicalDecomposition.lean`, `Unimodular3.lean`). This splits the problem into a full-rank part and a singular part, and the affine data transform correctly under the congruence, which is what makes the bound fboundG survive the change of coordinates.

*Full-rank case.* The fibre equation is a nondegenerate quadratic condition, and the dichotomy is proved at each fibre point rather than for the fibre as a whole. The top-level split is on the sign of α(2αc₀+β): non-positive forces c_{0} ≤ |β| and the point is bounded; positive, together with a signed non-square direction, produces an infinite orbit (Part I §§3–5). The case α = 0 is separate (§6), and the direction is produced by a sign trichotomy of the adjugate form, with the semidefinite alternative α·W ≥ 0 everywhere treated on its own (§§7–8). The classical isotropic–anisotropic distinction enters inside that last step rather than at the top: an isotropic form yields a line of solutions and a polynomial progression (Lemma V.3); an anisotropic one brings in the unit group of a real quadratic order (Lemma V.2), and a suitable initial solution of the shifted norm equation generates an infinite orbit under a unit preserving the congruence conditions the branch requires. (Nothing below needs the stronger classical statement that all solutions fall into finitely many such orbits; one infinite orbit suffices.) Iterating the unit produces the Pell-unit orbit of Definition 8.2(2), with the recurrence read off from multiplication by x₀ + y₀√D; the affine projection with denominator appears because the output coordinate is recovered from the orbit point only after clearing a fixed denominator (`FullRankAssemblyData.lean`).

*Singular case.* The form degenerates along its radical, and the reduction does **not** proceed by an induction on the number of unknowns. Part II dispatches on the rank k₀ of the nondegenerate quotient: k_{0} = 0 to Lemma 4, k_{0} = 1 to Lemma 6, k_{0} ≥ 2 to Lemma 5 (`QuotientRankZero.lean`, `QuotientRankOne.lean`). Underneath the dispatch sit the radical affine coefficients (`RadicalAffine.lean`), a nonzero-minor periodicity argument, a proportional compression, and the size transport back to the original affine data. The rank-zero leaf is the largest single case: all three of its infinite branches turn out to be progressions (an identity family, a periodic family, and a square progression).

*The moving-modulus branch.* One branch of the singular case resists both treatments: the fibre condition becomes a divisibility b·c + μ ∣ (a quadratic expression in the remaining unknowns), and the divisor moves with c. The step lemma shows that from a fibre point with positive modulus t = b·c + μ one can always find a prime p, congruent to 1 modulo F·b·t and larger than it, and a new fibre point whose modulus is exactly t·p (`moving_modulus_prime_step_data`). The congruence is what allows the quadratic residue condition to be transported to the new modulus; the size condition is what makes the sequence strictly increasing. Iterating from any base point with positive modulus gives the moving-modulus step orbit, with the modulus factor F pinned to 2|det G₀| in the singular-block branch — G₀ being the nondegenerate quotient block of Lemma 2, not the original Gram matrix, whose determinant is zero in that branch — and to 8·max(1,|D'|)·|2k| in the rank-one coupled branch. Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions (mathlib `Nat.infinite_setOf_prime_and_eq_mod`) supplies the prime at each step, and only there.

*Bounded branches.* Where no family is produced, the branch yields an explicit bound of the shape fboundG K ℓ q, and the constants are combined by taking the maximum over the finitely many branches. The branch table, with the kind emitted by each branch, ships with the formalization.

## Proposition 10.2

**Statement.** Let f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m}, m ≥ 1, be defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ whose form Q is nonzero, semidefinite of either sign, and of polar rank at most two. Then f has a weak vector coset normal form. In particular this holds for one output, one unknown and a positive definite form, for every n.

If R vanishes at every integer input, every solution is the centre and f is affine. Otherwise fix an integer input a_{0} with R(a_{0}) ≠ 0 and an integer solution z_{0} there; the off-centre vector z_{0} - c(a_{0}) gives a representation R(a_{0})/α = r_{0}^{2} + Δ s_{0}^{2}. The resulting affine family of solutions passes through z_{0} exactly; a common denominator M of its coefficients makes it integral on a_{0} + Mℤ^{n}, and output-uniqueness identifies its output coordinates with f there (`weakVectorCosetNF_of_binary`). No class-number argument and no uniqueness of the unknowns is used.

## Lemma 10.3

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `direction_discriminant_square`; standard axioms

**Statement.** Let Δ > 0, A ≠ 0, B, C be rational. If At^{2} + Bt + C is a value of X^{2} + ΔY^{2} with rational X, Y for every integer t, then B^{2} - 4AC = -Δh^{2} for some rational h. Consequently, if c + 2ℓ(x) + H(x), with ℓ a rational linear form and H a rational quadratic form on ℚ^{n}, is such a value at every x ∈ ℤ^{n}, then (cH(v) - ℓ(v)^{2})/Δ is the square of a rational number for every v ∈ ℤ^{n}.

**Proof.** Replacing Δ by an integer in its square class and multiplying the polynomial by a square, we may assume that Δ, A, B, C are integers; neither step changes the hypothesis or the conclusion. Put δ = B^{2} - 4AC. If δ = 0 the conclusion holds with h = 0. Suppose δ ≠ 0 and δ ≠ -Δh^{2} for every rational h. Then -Δ and -Δδ are not rational squares, so each is a quadratic non-residue on some unit residue class modulo a common period (`nonResidueClass_holds`); by the multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol one of the two classes or their product carries (-Δ/) = -1 and (δ/) = +1 (`characterSeparation_holds`), and Dirichlet's theorem gives arbitrarily large primes p in that class (`separating_prime_of_residueWitness`). Take such a p with p ∤ 2AΔδ. As δ is a nonzero square modulo p, the polynomial P(t) = At^{2} + Bt + C has a root t_{0} modulo p with P'(t_{0}) ≢ 0, and P(t_{0} + p) ≡ P(t_{0}) + pP'(t_{0}) {p^{2}}; so p divides one of P(t_{0}), P(t_{0} + p) exactly once. On the other hand a rational value X^{2} + ΔY^{2} has even p-adic valuation: after removing the common power of p from X and Y, a relation p ∣ X^{2} + ΔY^{2} with X, Y not both divisible by p would make -Δ a square modulo p. This contradiction proves the first statement. For the second, restrict to the line t ↦ tv: the value is c + 2ℓ(v)t + H(v)t^{2}. If H(v) ≠ 0, the first statement gives 4ℓ(v)^{2} - 4cH(v) = -Δh^{2}, that is (cH(v) - ℓ(v)^{2})/Δ = (h/2)^{2}. If H(v) = 0, the same valuation argument applied to the linear polynomial c + 2ℓ(v)t shows ℓ(v) = 0 (`not_total_of_linear`), and the quantity is 0.

## Lemma 10.4

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `square_values_rigidity`; standard axioms

**Statement.** Let T be a rational quadratic form on ℚ^{n} such that T(v) is the square of a rational number for every v ∈ ℤ^{n}. Then T = h^{2} for a rational linear form h.

**Proof.** Let N ≥ 1 clear the denominators of T and put A = N^{2}T. The values of A on ℤ^{n} are integers and rational squares, hence squares of integers. If A vanishes on ℤ^{n} it vanishes on ℚ^{n} by homogeneity, and h = 0. Otherwise fix w ∈ ℤ^{n} with A(w) = a^{2}, a > 0, and let B_{A}(x, y) = A(x + y) - A(x) - A(y). For v ∈ ℤ^{n} the polynomial p(t) = A(tw + v) = a^{2}t^{2} + B_{A}(w, v)t + A(v) is the square of an integer at every integer t. Let y_{t} ≥ 0 be its square root. For large t, y_{t} - at = (B_{A}(w, v)t + A(v))/(y_{t} + at) tends to B_{A}(w, v)/(2a); an integer sequence with a limit is eventually constant, so p(t) = (at + e)^{2} for some integer e and infinitely many t, hence identically. Comparing coefficients, A(v) = (B_{A}(w, v)/(2a))^{2}. Thus h(x) = B_{A}(w, x)/(2aN) satisfies T = h^{2} on ℤ^{n}, and on ℚ^{n} by homogeneity.

## Lemma 10.5

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `norm_section`; standard axioms

**Statement.** Let Δ > 0 and let r_{0}, s_{0} be rational with c = r_{0}^{2} + Δs_{0}^{2} > 0. If c + 2ℓ(x) + H(x) is a value of X^{2} + ΔY^{2} at every x ∈ ℤ^{n}, there are rational affine functions r, s on ℚ^{n} with (r(0), s(0)) = (r_{0}, s_{0}) and c + 2ℓ(x) + H(x) = r(x)^{2} + Δ s(x)^{2} for every x ∈ ℚ^{n}.

## Proposition 10.6

- head: `generalFullNF_iff_semidefiniteFullNF`, `isotropicBranchFullNF_holds`; standard axioms

**Statement.** Every total function defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ has a full vector coset normal form if and only if this holds for the equations with at least one output whose form Q is nonzero and semidefinite of either sign.

**Proof.** Use the case split of Theorem 4.2 (`branch_exhaustive`). If Q ≡ 0, the scalar relation behind the weak form already carries the full-residue form of the case without unknowns (`linearBranchFullNF_holds`). ## Lemma 10.8

**Statement.** Let S be a positive definite integral symmetric 3 × 3 matrix with ternary form Q_{S}, d ≥ 1 and ρ ∈ ℤ^{3}. There are finitely many primes and finitely many positive integers e such that, for every bound b on the valuations at those primes, there is N_{0} ≥ 1 with the following property: every integer N ≥ N_{0} that is primitively represented by ρ + dℤ^{3} modulo every prime power, satisfies v_{p}(N) ≤ b(p) at those primes and is not e times a rational square for any of the e, equals Q_{S}(ρ + dx) for some x ∈ ℤ^{3}. **Proof.** If a prime divides d and every coordinate of ρ, no integer is primitively represented by the coset modulo that prime, and the primitive statement {TCER} is empty for this coset. Otherwise d and the coordinates of ρ are coprime, and the coset is the lattice coset dL + ρ of conductor d in the sense of [KK26], for the integral lattice L = ℤ^{3} with bilinear form x^{}Sy. Its theta series splits as E + U + f. The coefficient of E at N is a product of local densities by Shimura's Siegel--Weil formula for lattice cosets, and it is at least c_{ε}N^{1/2 - ε} when N is locally represented by the coset and has bounded divisibility at finitely many bad primes; U is supported on finitely many square classes tℤ^{2}; and the coefficient of f is O(N^{3/7 + ε}) by Duke's bound. With ε = 1/56 the representation number of N outside the classes tℤ^{2} is at least cN^{27/56} - CN^{25/56} = N^{25/56}(cN^{1/28} - C), which is positive once N > (2C/c)^{28}; here c > 0 and C depend only on the coset and the bound b, and no effective value is claimed. Take the bad primes of [KK26] and let the e be the t. A solution modulo every power of p gives a p-adic solution by compactness of ℤ_{p}^{3}, so primitive representability modulo every prime power implies local representability; excluding every class eℚ^{2} excludes at least the classes tℤ^{2}; and N_{0} may be taken ≥ 1. The order of quantifiers --- coset, then bad primes and exceptional classes, then the valuation bound, then the threshold --- is the order in [KK26].

## Proposition 10.9

**Statement.** Assume {TCER} and let m ≥ 2. A function f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m} is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ, with any finite number of unknowns, if and only if it has a common slope.

**Proof.** Only the forward direction uses {TCER} (`sealedCommonSlopeNF_of_TCER`). With m ≥ 2 the form Q is nonzero, since a relation with Q ≡ 0 is output-unique only when m ≤ 1, and its polar rank d is at least m. In the isotropic branch with d ≥ 3 no input with an affine centre has an integer solution: if the off-centre vector x of a solution is radical, a shift by an integral multiple Ne_{0} of a non-radical isotropic vector keeps Q(x), gives a solution at the same input and makes the off-centre vector non-radical, and the Eichler argument of Proposition 10.6 applies; so this branch contains no function (`isotropic_three_le_polarRank_not_defines`). With d ≤ 2 the residual is constant (Proposition 10.6) and the constant-residual transport gives a common slope. In the quotient-anisotropic indefinite case f is globally affine, and its rational coefficients are the integers f(0) and f(e_{i}) - f(0) (`intAffine_of_ratAffine`). In the semidefinite case the positive definite reduction and {TCER} give a constant residual, hence a common slope, before any full form is extracted.

The backward direction holds for every m without {TCER} (`singleQuadratic_of_commonSlope`). Put β_{ρ} = f(ρ) for ρ ∈ {0, , M - 1}^{n} and δ_{ρ} = Mβ_{ρ} - Bρ. With unknowns u ∈ ℤ^{n} and e ∈ ℤ^{M^{n}} the equation

(_{ρ} e_{ρ} - 1)^{2} + _{ρ} e_{ρ}(e_{ρ} - 1) + a - Mu - _{ρ} e_{ρ}ρ^{2} + Mb - Ba - _{ρ} e_{ρ}δ_{ρ}^{2} = 0

has total degree two, and every summand is non-negative on the integers. A solution therefore has e equal to a unit vector at some ρ, a = Mu + ρ and Mb = M(β_{ρ} + Bu), so b = f(ρ + Mu) = f(a); conversely Euclidean division of a by M gives a solution with b = f(a), and its witnesses are unique (`slopeEncoder_graph_iff`, `slopeEncoder_witness_unique`). ## Lemma 10.13

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `addSubgroup_two_coset_cover`

**Statement.** Let H and K be subgroups of an additive commutative group G and b ∈ G. If every element of G lies in exactly one of H and b + K, then H = K, b ∉ H, every element lies in H or in b + H, and g + g ∈ H for every g ∈ G.

## Proposition 10.14

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `scalar_singleQuadratic_iff_of_TCER`

**Statement.** Assume {TCER}. A function f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ, with any finite number of unknowns, if and only if one of the following holds: f has a common slope; f agrees on ℤ^{n} with a polynomial with rational coefficients of total degree at most two; or there are integer vectors p, q and integers c, c', with p - q having even coordinates, p having an odd coordinate and c - c' odd, such that 2f(a) = p · a + c when p · a + c is even and 2f(a) = q · a + c' otherwise.

**Proof.** The reverse direction holds without {TCER} (`singleQuadratic_of_scalarFamilies`). A common slope is compiled as in Proposition 10.9. For a rational polynomial q with common denominator N the equation Nb - Nq(a) = 0 has no unknowns. In the third case (p - q) · a + (c - c') is odd for every a, so exactly one of p · a + c and q · a + c' is even, and (2b - p · a - c)(2b - q · a - c') = 0 holds exactly at b = f(a); again there are no unknowns.

Only the forward direction uses {TCER} (`sealed_scalarFamilies_of_TCER`). If Q ≢ 0 and the polar rank is at least two, the branch arguments in the proof of Proposition 10.9 use only m > 0 and give a common slope (`commonSlope_of_TCER_of_two_le_polarRank`). Otherwise the equation reduces to αy^{2} + (β · x + d)y + T(x) = 0. If α = 0, f is a rational polynomial of degree at most two. If α ≠ 0, then at each integer x the equation has the integer root y = f(x), so Δ(x) = (β · x + d)^{2} - 4αT(x) = (2αf(x) + β · x + d)^{2} is a square at every integer point. A quadratic polynomial that is a square at every integer point is the square of an affine function L with rational coefficients (`c21_affine_square`); since L(x) = ±(2αf(x) + β · x + d) is an integer for every integer x, the constant s = L(0) and the coefficients ℓ_{i} = L(e_{i}) - L(0) are integers. Put u(x) = -(β · x + d) + (ℓ · x + s) and v(x) = -(β · x + d) - (ℓ · x + s). Then 4α(αy^{2} + (β · x + d)y + T(x)) = (2αy - u(x))(2αy - v(x)), so for all integers x and y the equation holds if and only if 2αy = u(x) or 2αy = v(x) (`rn_two_roots`). Let I_{u} and I_{v} be the sets where 2α divides u or v; on I_{u} the quotient is a solution, so 2αf = u there, and likewise on I_{v}, and I_{u} ∪ I_{v} = ℤ^{n}. If I_{u} and I_{v} meet at x_{0}, both divisibilities hold on x_{0} + 2αℤ^{n}, so u = v there, hence everywhere (`affine_eq_of_eq_on_coset`), and f is affine; the same holds if one of the sets is empty. Otherwise I_{u} and I_{v} are disjoint cosets of {h : 2α ∣ U · h} and {h : 2α ∣ V · h}, where U and V are the linear parts of u and v. By Lemma 10.13 these subgroups coincide and contain 2ℤ^{n}, so α divides the coordinates of U, V and the constants; writing u = α(p · x + c) and v = α(q · x + c'), the two sets are where p · x + c, respectively q · x + c', is even. Exactly one of them holds at 0 and at each unit vector, which gives the parity conditions, and a proper non-empty set forces an odd coordinate of p (`rn_classify`).

**Statement.** Assume {TCER}. For every f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m}, the map a ↦ (f(a), 0) ∈ ℤ^{m} × ℤ is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ if and only if f has a common slope.

## Proposition 10.16

**Proof.** The reverse direction is the construction in the proof of Proposition 10.14, which uses no unknowns. ## Proposition 10.17

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `singleQuadratic_iff_uniqueWitness_of_TCER`

**Statement.** Assume {TCER}. A function f : ℤ^{n} → ℤ^{m} is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ if and only if it is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ whose solutions in the unknowns coincide whenever the inputs and outputs do. In particular each input then has exactly one solution in the output and the unknowns together.

**Proof.** The reverse direction is immediate. For the forward direction, with m ≠ 1 the function has a common slope by Proposition 10.9, the case m = 0 being trivial; with m = 1 it has a common slope or is of one of the other two kinds of Proposition 10.14. In the equation of the proof of Proposition 10.9 the degree-two part in the output and the unknowns is a sum of squares that vanishes only at zero, and a solution determines e, as the unit vector at the residue ρ of a modulo M, and then u (`commonSlope_posDef_presentation`, `uniqueWitness_of_commonSlope`). The other two kinds are defined without unknowns (Proposition 10.16). Each input has a solution by totality, its output is unique, and then so are the unknowns (`existsUnique_of_uniqueWitness`). Only the forward direction uses {TCER}.

## Proposition 10.18

- source: `10_s10_definable_functions.tex`  
- head: `floorThird_min_witnesses`

**Statement.** The map ℤ → ℤ, a ↦ ⌊a/3⌋, is defined by a single quadratic equation over ℤ with one unknown, and by no single quadratic equation over ℤ without unknowns.

**Proof.** The equation (3b - a + 1)^{2} + u^{2} - 1 = 0 has one unknown u. If (3b - a + 1)^{2} + u^{2} = 1 then |3b - a + 1| ≤ 1, so a - 2 ≤ 3b ≤ a and b = ⌊a/3⌋; conversely, for a = 3q + r with 0 ≤ r < 3 take b = q and u = 1 if r = 1, u = 0 otherwise (`floorThird_oneWitness`). At a = 1, b = 0 both u = 1 and u = -1 are solutions, so the unknown is not unique in this presentation (`floorThirdPoly_two_witnesses`). Without unknowns, Proposition 10.16 would make a ↦ ⌊a/3⌋ a rational polynomial of degree at most two or of the third kind of Proposition 10.14. The first is impossible: such a polynomial would vanish at 0, 1 and 2 and take the value 1 at 3. In the second, the coefficient p is odd, since n = 1; so one of x = 0 and x = 1 has px + c even, and then so has x + 6, which gives 6p = 2(⌊(x + 6)/3⌋ - ⌊x/3⌋) = 4, impossible in integers (`floorThird_not_zeroWitness`). No {TCER} is used.

## Theorem C.1

- source: `17_appc_the_multi_parameter_universal_pair_quest.tex`  
- head: `coder_counting_bound_weighted`, `multiUniversalN_bound_weighted`, `multiUniversalZ_bound_weighted`, `wtCount_closed_form`; standard axioms

**Statement.** Let ν, δ : ℕ → ℕ and suppose that for every p, every Diophantine subset of ℕ^{p} is defined by a single equation in ν(p) unknowns of total degree at most δ(p), with unknowns ranging over ℕ or over ℤ. Then for every p ≥ 1,


p − 1 ≤ {wtCount}\ δ(p)\ (ν(p) + 1) = C(δ(p) + ν(p) + 1, ν(p) + 2), 
where {wtCount}\ d\ k := Σ_{|m| ≤ d, m in k variables} (d − |m|).

**Proof.** Fix n and set p = n + 1.

*(1) The coder, and what universality is applied to.* Define e : ℕ^{n} → ℕ by iterating Cantor's pairing bijection ⟨·,·⟩ : ℕ^{2} → ℕ: e of the empty tuple is 0, and e(a_{1}, …, a_{k+1}) = ⟨e(a_{1}, …, a_{k}), a_{k+1}⟩ (`code`). Section 2 defines no coder; earlier versions of this appendix cited one there, and that reference was wrong rather than merely terse. e is injective, because ⟨·,·⟩ is and injectivity at arity k lifts to arity k + 1 in both components (`code_injective`). Its graph {(a, c) ∈ ℕⁿ⁺¹ : c = e(a)} is a Diophantine subset of ℕᵖ for p = n + 1, the pairing relation being cut out by a polynomial equation and the iteration being a finite conjunction of such (`diophN_graph_code`). The hypothesis of the theorem quantifies over every Diophantine subset of ℕᵖ, so it applies to this graph in particular. The hypothesis at p supplies an integer polynomial C in the p parameters (a, c) and r ≤ ν(p) unknowns u, of total degree d ≤ δ(p), such that (a, c) lies in the graph if and only if C(a, c, u) = 0 has a solution u in the admissible range. It is the *graph* that is represented, not a subset of ℕⁿ; this is where the extra parameter c comes from, and it is why the fibre below has 1 + r variables rather than r.

*(2) Specialisation.* For a ∈ ℕ^{n} let Φ(a) := C(a, ·, ·) ∈ ℤ[c, u_{1}, …, u_{r}], the polynomial in 1 + r variables obtained by substituting the numbers a for the first n parameters. Its total degree is at most d.

*(3) Φ is injective.* Suppose Φ(a) = Φ(a′). Because e is total and single-valued, (a, e(a)) lies in the graph, so there is an admissible u with C(a, e(a), u) = 0, that is, Φ(a) vanishes at (e(a), u). Then Φ(a′) vanishes there too, so (a′, e(a)) lies in the graph, so e(a′) = e(a). Injectivity of e now gives a′ = a. Totality and single-valuedness are what produce the witness; injectivity is used only at this last step, to turn equal outputs into equal inputs.

*(4) Coefficient growth over a box, graded by monomial degree.* Write Φ(a) = Σ_{|m| ≤ d} f_{m}(a) · z^{m}, where z = (c, u) and m runs over the monomials of degree at most d in the 1 + r fibre variables. Each f_{m} is a polynomial in a with integer coefficients, and since C has total degree d and zᵐ already carries |m| of it, f_{m} has degree at most d − |m|. Let K := Σ_β |coeff_β(C)| be the ℓ¹ size of the fixed polynomial C, a finite number determined by C alone. For a in the box [0, N]ⁿ with N ≥ 1 this gives


|f_{m}(a)| ≤ K · N^{d − |m|}

(`aux_coeff_bound_weighted`). The exponent drops with |m|: a monomial that has already spent |m| on the fibre variables can receive contributions only from those terms of C whose parameter part has degree at most d − |m|.

*(5) The count.* By (4) the coefficient at m takes at most 2K·N^{d−|m|} + 1 integer values, and since N ≥ 1 this is at most (2K + 1)·N^{d−|m|}. Multiplying over the M := C(d + r + 1, r + 1) monomials, the number of distinct specialisations is at most (2K + 1)^{M} · N^{W}, where W := Σ_{|m| ≤ d} (d − |m|) = {wtCount} d (r + 1). By (3) the (N + 1)ⁿ points of the box give pairwise distinct specialisations, so

(N + 1)^{n} ≤ (2K + 1)^{M} · N^{W}

(`aux_counting_weighted`). Suppose n ≥ W + 1 and take N := (2K + 1)^{M} + 1. Then (2K + 1)^{M} · N^{W} < N · N^{W} = N^{W+1} ≤ N^{n} < (N + 1)^{n}, contradicting the display. Two edges of the count deserve a sentence, since a reader checking it will look for them. *Vanishing coefficients.* If some f_{m} is identically zero the display still holds: the range counted at m is the integer interval of radius K·N^{d−|m|}, which contains 0, so a coefficient that takes one value is counted among the at most 2K·N^{d−|m|} + 1 that it may take. Nothing in (4) or (5) assumes f_{m} ≠ 0. *Degree zero.* If d = 0 then C is a constant, W = {wtCount} 0 (r + 1) = C(r + 1, r + 2) = 0, and the bound reads n ≤ 0: a constant equation represents no coder of positive arity, which is the right conclusion and is delivered by the same inequality rather than by a separate case. Hence n ≤ W = {wtCount} d (r + 1), and since wtCount is monotone in both arguments (`wtCount_mono`) and d ≤ δ(p), r ≤ ν(p), we get n ≤ {wtCount} δ(p) (ν(p) + 1), which is the assertion at p = n + 1.

*Closed form.* For k ≥ 1, which is the case here since k = r + 1, the number of monomials of degree exactly j in k variables is C(j + k − 1, k − 1), so W = Σ_{j=0}^{d} (d − j)·C(j + k − 1, k − 1). Writing d − j as the number of i with j ≤ i ≤ d − 1 and exchanging the order of summation, W = Σ_{i=0}^{d−1} Σ_{j=0}^{i} C(j + k − 1, k − 1) = Σ_{i=0}^{d−1} C(i + k, k) = C(d + k, k + 1), by the hockey-stick identity applied twice. The Lean statement `wtCount_closed_form` is proved for every k, including k = 0, where the displayed count of monomials does not apply and W = d = C(d, 1) directly.

## Remark C.2

- source: `17_appc_the_multi_parameter_universal_pair_quest.tex`  
- head: the weighted and the crude bound

**Statement.** The unweighted form of the obstruction (`coder_counting_bound`) bounds every coefficient by K·N^{d}, uniformly in the monomial, and so pays d for each of the M monomials, giving the cruder n ≤ d·M. Step (4) pays only d − |m|, which turns a product of equal factors into a sum of unequal ones. That the weighted bound refines the crude one and is not a competing claim is a theorem: `wtCount_le_crude` proves {wtCount} d k ≤ d · monoCount d k. The bound as stated, in terms of wtCount, and both closed forms are verified in the formal artifact: `wtCount_closed_form` proves {wtCount} d k = C(d + k, k + 1) and `monoCount_closed_form` proves the companion identity monoCount d k = C(d + k, k). The comparison with Theorem 4.1 is that both are box counts, and the difference is which box: there the images of a parameter box are confined by the growth theorem, here the specialisations of a fixed polynomial over a parameter box are confined by the size of its own coefficients.

## Theorem (unnumbered)

- source: `20_appf_the_arithmetic_projection_theorem_comple.tex`  
- head: full-rank projection dichotomy

**Statement.** If {det} G ≠ 0 there is K_{1} = K_{1}(A,B,C) such that for every (l,m,q): S(l,m,q) is infinite, or every c ∈ S(l,m,q) satisfies c ≤ K_{1}·((1+|ℓ|_{1})^{2}+|q|).

**Proof.** The proof shape is a per-point alternative:

every c_{0} ∈ S either satisfies a direct bound c₀ ≤ |β|-type, or generates, through one of its own witnesses u₀, an infinite family of fiber elements.

If S is not infinite, the second alternative is impossible at every point, so every point satisfies the direct bound. No global line restriction of the fiber occurs anywhere in the argument, and no boundedness statement is ever transported from a subfamily to S.

{1. The completion identity}
For every (c,u) ∈ ℤ × ℤ^{r},

2N·F(c,u) = W(Y(c,u)) + α c^{2} + β c + γ. (1.1)

Both sides are integer polynomials in (c,u); the identity is checked by expanding, using G·{adj}(G) = N·I and the symmetry of adj(G). The factor 2N is a nonzero scalar multiplier, not a unit of ℤ: it is never divided by. The one cancellation used downstream is of the form 2N·x = 0 ⇒ x = 0 with N ≠ 0, which holds because ℤ is an integral domain. In particular F(c,u) = 0 ⟺ W(Y) + αc^{2} + βc + γ = 0.

{2. The scaled line through a fiber point}
Let (c_{0}, u_{0}) ∈ ℤ × ℤ^{r} and let w ∈ ℤ^{r} be any direction. The scaled line through (c₀, u₀) in direction w is the two-parameter integer family

c(t) = c_{0} + N t, u(t,s) = u_{0} + s·{adj}(G)·w − t·{adj}(G)·B, (t,s) ∈ ℤ^{2}. (2.1)

Every point of the family lies in ℤ × ℤ^{r} because adj(G) is an integer matrix; this is the integrality mechanism N·ℤ^{r} ⊆ G·ℤ^{r} in action, since G·({adj}(G)·d) = N·d for every d ∈ ℤ^{r}.

*Lattice transport.* Along (2.1) the completed vector moves in the single direction w:

Y(c(t), u(t,s)) = Y_{0} + (N s)·w, where Y_{0} = Y(c_{0},u_{0}). (2.2)

The c-dependence of Y cancels against the −t·adj(G)·B term: G·(t·adj(G)·B) = Nt·B absorbs the increment (Nt)·B of the cB summand.

*Line value.* Substituting (2.2) into (1.1) and expanding the square,

2N·F(c(t), u(t,s)) = 2N·F(c_{0},u_{0}) + N·[ Nα·t² + N·W̃(w)·s² + Z₀·t + 2B₀·s ], (2.3)

where Z_{0} = 2α c_{0} + β and B_{0} = Y_{0}ᵀ·{adj}(G)·w. The bracket is a binary integer quadratic in (t,s) with no ts-term, no constant term, t²-coefficient Nα and s²-coefficient N·W̃(w). If (c₀,u₀) is a fiber solution, F(c_{0},u_{0}) = 0, so the zeros of F on the line are exactly the integer zeros of the bracket, and (t,s) = (0,0) is one of them.

{3. The bounded alternative at a point: α(2αc_{0}+β) ≤ 0 forces c_{0} ≤ |β|}
Let α ≠ 0 and let c_{0} ∈ S, c_{0} ≥ 0, with α·(2α c_{0} + β) ≤ 0. Then 2α^{2}c_{0} ≤ −αβ ≤ |α|·|β|. Since |α| ≥ 1 and c_{0} ≥ 0, |α|·c_{0} ≤ α^{2}·c_{0}, hence |α|·2c_{0} ≤ 2α^{2}c_{0} ≤ |α|·|β|; cancelling |α| > 0 gives 2c_{0} ≤ |β| and in particular

c_{0} ≤ |β|. (3.1)

This step uses nothing about witnesses: it is a sign computation on the linear polynomial 2αc₀ + β. The passage from |β| to fbound is the size estimate |β| ≤ kβ·(1+|ℓ|_{1}) with the explicit constant kβ = 2|N| + 2·Σ_{ij}|B_{i}·{adj}(G)_{ij}|, together with (1+|ℓ|_{1}) ≤ (1+|ℓ|_{1})^{2} + |q|.

{4. The infinite alternative at a point: α(2αc_{0}+β) > 0 with a signed non-square direction}
Call w ∈ ℤ^{r} a *signed non-square direction* for α when

α·W(w) < 0 and −α·W̃(w) is not a square in ℤ. (4.1)

{proposition*}
Let α ≠ 0, let w satisfy (4.1), and let c_{0} ∈ S with witness u₀ and α·Z_{0} > 0, where Z_{0} = 2αc_{0} + β. Then S is infinite.

{proposition*}
{proof}
Consider the scaled line (2.1) through (c₀,u₀) in direction w, and the binary quadratic of (2.3):

Q(t,s) = Nα·t^{2} + N·W(w)·s^{2} + Z_{0}·t + 2B_{0}·s.

Write ε = sign(N) ∈ {±1}, so that N·ε = |N| ≥ 1. The following six facts drive the Pell mechanism.

(i) *Discriminant positivity.* disc(Q) with respect to its quadratic part (Nα, 0, N·W̃(w)) equals 0^{2} − 4·Nα·N·W(w) = (2N)^{2}·(−α·W(w)) > 0 by (4.1).

(ii) *Discriminant non-squareness.* A square times a non-square is a non-square: (2N)^{2} ≠ 0 and −α·W̃(w) non-square give disc(Q) non-square.

(iii) *Initial point.* Q(0,0) = 0: the auxiliary one-witness fiber set of the form (Nα, 0, N·W̃(w)) with affine data (ε·Z₀, 2B₀, 0) contains t = 0.

(iv) *Orientation.* The Pell entry condition at the initial point is the positivity of X₀ = 2·disc(Q)·0 + (2·0·2B_{0} − 4·N·W(w)·ε·Z_{0}) = −4·|N|·W(w)·Z_{0}. From α·W(w) < 0 and α·Z_{0} > 0, the product α²·W̃(w)·Z₀ is negative, and α^{2} > 0 forces W(w)·Z_{0} < 0; hence X_{0} > 0.

(v) *Infinite orbit.* By (i)–(iv), the Pell automorph of the binary form (Nα, 0, N·W̃(w)) — powered from a nontrivial positive solution of p² − disc·q² = 1, which exists because disc(Q) is positive and non-square — moves the initial point to solutions with strictly increasing t-height: the auxiliary fiber set contains infinitely many t ∈ ℕ. This is the one-witness Pell theorem of the two-variable layer, applied verbatim to the auxiliary form.

(vi) *Transport back, distinctness, nonnegativity, residue.* Each auxiliary solution t = z′ ∈ ℕ (with its s) yields, through t := ε·z′ in (2.1) and the identity (2.3) with 2N cancelled, a genuine fiber solution: the sign adjustment ε in the affine coefficient of (iii) and in t := ε·z′ cancel through ε^{2} = 1, landing at

c = c_{0} + N·(ε·z′) = c_{0} + |N|·z′ ∈ ℕ.

The map z′ ↦ c_{0} + |N|·z′ is injective (|N| ≥ 1), its outputs are nonnegative, and they all lie in the residue class c₀ mod |N|. The image of an infinite set under an injective map is infinite, so S is infinite.

## Lemma (unnumbered)

## Lemma V.1
